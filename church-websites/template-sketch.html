Why it stays current

Most church sites die because the one volunteer who built them moved on. Here the Facebook posts show on the site by themselves, so a church that posts on Facebook already has a living website. The pastor only edits the few things that change: the banner, times, phone and welcome.

Editing from a phone

Eight plain boxes and one Publish button. He can't break the layout: the design is fixed, and only the words change. Sign-in is a code by text message, so there's no password to lose when someone moves on; a second person can be added.

No spam

  • No email address on the page, so spammers have nothing to collect.
  • The contact form carries a hidden trap field that only robots fill in, plus a free "are you human" check that most people never see (such as Cloudflare Turnstile).
  • Messages go to the pastor's email; his address stays private.

Cost and ownership

The site is plain pages with nothing to hack, so it can sit on a free hosting plan. The church pays only for its own web address (about $10–20 a year) and owns it, so the site survives any change of helper.

One template, many churches

Each church fills in the same template: name, town, times, pastor, phone, Facebook, statement of faith. Setting one up is about an hour; the 31 churches from the list with no site, a broken one or a dated one could each have one.

Ties to the Bible app

Every site shows the Verse of the Day and a link to read the King James Bible. Together the sites could also feed a simple "Find a King James church near you" page, built from the same list.
